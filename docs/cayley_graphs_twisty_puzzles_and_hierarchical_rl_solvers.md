# Cayley Graphs, Combinatorial Twisty Puzzles, and Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning Solvers

## Part I: Foundations of Cayley Graphs

A **Cayley graph** (introduced by Arthur Cayley in 1878) is a combinatorial and geometric object that encodes the abstract algebraic structure of a group. It serves as the foundational bridge between group theory, graph theory, and metric geometry—turning algebraic questions about group presentations and word problems into geometric questions about paths, cycles, and expansion.

### 1. Formal Definition & Conventions

Let $G$ be a group and $S \subseteq G$ be a subset of elements (almost always chosen to be a **generating set** of $G$). The Cayley graph $\Gamma = \text{Cay}(G, S)$ is constructed as follows:

* **Vertices:** The vertex set $V(\Gamma)$ is the set of elements of $G$.

* **Edges:** For every $g \in G$ and $s \in S$, there is a directed edge of "color" $s$ from $g$ to $gs$ (right multiplication convention).

  * *Why right multiplication?* Multiplying vertices on the **left** by an element $h \in G$ maps the edge $(g, gs)$ to $(hg, hgs)$, which remains a valid edge of color $s$. Thus, left multiplication acts on the graph by **color-preserving graph automorphisms**.

**Two Standard Variants:**

1. **Directed and Edge-Colored:** Each generator $s \in S$ receives a distinct color and direction, faithfully preserving the exact algebraic presentation.

2. **Undirected and Uncolored (Geometric Convention):** We assume the identity $e \notin S$ (removing self-loops) and that $S$ is **symmetric** ($S = S^{-1}$, meaning if $s \in S$, then $s^{-1} \in S$). Each pair of directed edges $(g, gs)$ and $(gs, g)$ is collapsed into a single undirected edge.

### 2. Fundamental Structural Properties

* **Connectivity:** $\text{Cay}(G, S)$ is connected if and only if $S$ generates $G$ ($\langle S \rangle = G$). If $S$ does not generate $G$, the connected components correspond to the cosets of the subgroup $\langle S \rangle$.

* **Regularity:** Every vertex has exact degree $\vert{}S\vert{}$ (in the symmetric undirected case). If $S$ is finite, the graph is **locally finite**.

* **Vertex-Transitivity:** For any two vertices $u, v \in G$, the map $x \mapsto (vu^{-1})x$ is a graph automorphism mapping $u$ to $v$. Visually, the graph looks identical from every vertex.

* **Cycles and Relations:** Closed walks in $\text{Cay}(G, S)$ correspond to words in the generators $S$ that evaluate to the identity element $e \in G$. Consequently:

  * $\text{Cay}(G, S)$ is a **tree** if and only if $G$ is a **free group** freely generated by $S$ (no non-trivial relations exist).

  * Taking quotients by normal subgroups corresponds to folding the Cayley graph by identifying vertices and creating cycles out of relations.

* **Sabidussi’s Theorem (1958):** A graph $\Gamma$ is a Cayley graph of some group $G$ if and only if its automorphism group $\text{Aut}(\Gamma)$ contains a subgroup isomorphic to $G$ that acts **regularly** (sharply transitively—transitively and freely) on the vertex set. (Note: Not every vertex-transitive graph is a Cayley graph; the **Petersen graph** is the classic 10-vertex counterexample).

### 3. Canonical Examples

| Group $G$ | Generating Set $S$ | Resulting Cayley Graph $\text{Cay}(G, S)$ | 
| ----- | ----- | ----- | 
| **Cyclic Group** $\mathbb{Z}_n$ | $\{\pm 1\}$ | Cycle graph $C_n$ | 
| **Integers** $\mathbb{Z}$ | $\{\pm 1\}$ | Infinite path (discrete number line) | 
| **Free Abelian** $\mathbb{Z}^2$ | $\{(\pm 1, 0), (0, \pm 1)\}$ | Infinite 2D square grid | 
| **Elementary Abelian** $\mathbb{Z}_2^d$ | Standard basis vectors $e_1, \dots, e_d$ | $d$-dimensional **Hypercube** $Q_d$ | 
| **Symmetric Group** $S_n$ | Adjacent transpositions $(i, i+1)$ | $(n-1)$-dimensional **Permutohedron** | 
| **Free Group** $F_2 = \langle a, b \rangle$ | $\{a^{\pm 1}, b^{\pm 1}\}$ | Infinite 4-regular tree (Bethe lattice) | 
| **Dihedral Group** $D_n$ | $\{r, r^{-1}, s\}$ (rotation & flip) | $n$-sided **Prism graph** | 
| **Any Group** $G$ | $G \setminus \{e\}$ | Complete graph $K_{\vert{}G\vert{}}$ | 

### 4. Metric & Geometric Group Theory

In **Geometric Group Theory** (pioneered by Mikhail Gromov), finitely generated infinite groups are studied by treating their Cayley graphs as metric spaces.

* **The Word Metric:** For any $g, h \in G$, the word metric $d_S(g, h)$ is the length of the shortest path between $g$ and $h$ in $\text{Cay}(G, S)$:
  

  $$
  d_S(g, h) = \Vert{}g^{-1}h\Vert{}_S = \min \{ k \ge 0 \mid g^{-1}h = s_1 s_2 \cdots s_k, \, s_i \in S \}
  $$

* **Quasi-Isometry:** If $S_1$ and $S_2$ are two finite generating sets of $G$, the metric spaces $(\text{Cay}(G, S_1), d_{S_1})$ and $(\text{Cay}(G, S_2), d_{S_2})$ are **quasi-isometric** (bi-Lipschitz equivalent). Large-scale geometric properties belong to the group itself:

  * **Growth Rate:** Let $\beta_S(r)$ be the number of elements in a ball of radius $r$ around $e$. By **Gromov’s Theorem (1981)**, a finitely generated group has polynomial growth if and only if it is **virtually nilpotent**. Free groups have exponential growth, while **Grigorchuk’s Group** exhibits intermediate growth.

  * **Hyperbolicity:** A group is **Gromov hyperbolic** if all geodesic triangles in its Cayley graph are $\delta$-thin. Hyperbolic groups have a linear-time solvable Word Problem via Dehn's algorithm.

  * **Ends of a Group (Freudenthal–Hopf & Stallings' Theorems):** A locally finite Cayley graph can only have $0$, $1$, $2$, or $\infty$ ends.

### 5. Spectral Properties, Expanders, and Open Problems

* **Representation Theory & Eigenvalues:** The adjacency matrix $A$ of $\text{Cay}(G, S)$ corresponds to the action of $\sum_{s \in S} s$ in the regular representation $\mathbb{C}[G]$. For abelian groups, eigenvalues are explicit character sums $\lambda_\chi = \sum_{s \in S} \chi(s)$. For non-abelian groups, the spectrum decomposes into blocks corresponding to irreducible representations.

* **Expander Graphs:** A family of $d$-regular Cayley graphs $\text{Cay}(G_n, S_n)$ is an expander family if the spectral gap $d - \lambda_2$ remains bounded away from zero as $\vert{}G_n\vert{} \to \infty$. Non-abelian simple groups (such as Lubotzky-Phillips-Sarnak **Ramanujan graphs** from $PSL_2(\mathbb{F}_q)$) create optimal expanders where random walks mix in $O(\log \vert{}G\vert{})$ steps.

* **Major Open Problems:**

  1. **The Lovász Hamiltonian Cycle Conjecture:** Does every finite connected Cayley graph ($\vert{}G\vert{} \ge 3$) contain a Hamiltonian cycle ("Devil's Algorithm")?

  2. **Babai’s Conjecture on Diameter:** For any non-abelian finite simple group $G$ and *any* generating set $S$, is $\text{diam}(\text{Cay}(G, S)) \le (\log \vert{}G\vert{})^{O(1)}$?

## Part II: Modeling Twisty Puzzles & Training Heuristic Search (DeepCubeA)

Solving a twisty puzzle is equivalent to finding a shortest path from a scrambled state $g \in G$ to the identity vertex $e$ in $\text{Cay}(G, S)$.

### 1. Word Metrics as Puzzle Move Metrics

Changing the generating set $S \subset G$ changes the move metric and reshapes the Cayley graph:

| Metric Name | Generating Set $S$ ($3\times 3\times 3$ Cube) | Vertex Degree $\Vert{}S\Vert{}$ | Diameter $\text{diam}(\text{Cay}(G, S))$ | Bipartite? | 
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | 
| **Quarter-Turn Metric (QTM)** | $90^\circ$ turns $\{U^{\pm 1}, D^{\pm 1}, L^{\pm 1}, R^{\pm 1}, F^{\pm 1}, B^{\pm 1}\}$ | $12$ | $26$ | **Yes** (odd permutation parity per move) | 
| **Half-Turn Metric (HTM)** | QTM generators plus $180^\circ$ turns $\{U^2, D^2, L^2, R^2, F^2, B^2\}$ | $18$ | $20$ | **No** ($180^\circ$ turns are even permutations) | 
| **Slice-Turn Metric (STM)** | HTM generators plus slice turns $\{M^{\pm 1}, M^2, E^{\pm 1}, E^2, S^{\pm 1}, S^2\}$ | $27$ | $18$ (or $19$) | **No** | 

* **Schreier Coset Graphs for Indistinguishable Pieces:** If a puzzle has identical pieces (such as $4\times 4\times 4$ centers or symmetric mono-colored gears), the state space is a **Schreier coset graph** $\text{Sch}(G, H, S)$, where $H \le G$ is the stabilizer subgroup of the solved visual state.

### 2. Branching Factors & Perron–Frobenius Eigenvalues

Naive search has raw branching factor $b_{\text{raw}} = \vert{}S\vert{}$ ($18$ in HTM, $12$ in QTM), but local algebraic relations create trivial 2-cycles ($U \cdot U^{-1} = e$) and 4-cycles (commuting opposite faces $U D = D U$). Imposing a canonical ordering on commuting faces models valid non-redundant paths as a finite-state Markov transition matrix $M$:

* **In HTM:**
  

  $$
  M_{\text{HTM}} = \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 3 \\ 12 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies \lambda^2 - 12\lambda - 36 = 0 \implies b_{\text{eff, HTM}} = 6 + 6\sqrt{2} \approx 14.48528
  $$

* **In QTM:**
  

  $$
  M_{\text{QTM}} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 2 \\ 8 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies \lambda^2 - 8\lambda - 16 = 0 \implies b_{\text{eff, QTM}} = 4 + 4\sqrt{2} \approx 9.65685
  $$

### 3. Concentric Sphere Distribution & Diameter ("God's Number")

In expander-like non-abelian Cayley graphs, sphere volume $S(r) = \{g \in G : d_S(g, e) = r\}$ grows at $\sim b_{\text{eff}}^r$ until abruptly exhausting the group near the diameter. For the $3\times 3\times 3$ cube in HTM ($\vert{}G\vert{} \approx 4.33 \times 10^{19}$), roughly **97.3%** of all states lie in the shell $d_S(g, e) \in \{17, 18\}$, while fewer than $10^{-9}\%$ of states sit at the maximum antipodal distance of $20$.

### 4. DeepCubeA: Approximate Value Iteration on $\text{Cay}(G, S)$

While classical IDA\* relies on **Pattern Databases (PDBs)** (precomputing exact distances in small quotient projections $\text{Sch}(G, H, S)$), PDBs break down when puzzle orbits are tightly coupled. **DeepCubeA** (Agostinelli et al., 2019) trains a deep residual network $f_\theta: G \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ to approximate the exact word metric $d_S(x, e)$ directly:

1. **Reverse-Walk Data Generation:** Exploiting vertex-transitivity and invertibility, DeepCubeA starts at $x_0 = e$ and takes $K$-step random walks $x_k = x_{k-1}s_k$ ($K \sim \text{Uniform}(1, K_{\max})$), sampling across all concentric shells around $e$.

2. **Bellman Optimality as the Word Metric Triangle Inequality:** The word metric $d_S(x, e)$ is the unique fixed point of the deterministic Bellman operator. Using a target network $f_{\theta^-}$, **Deep Approximate Value Iteration (DAVI)** minimizes:
   

   $$
   y_i = \min_{s \in S} \big( 1 + f_{\theta^-}(x_i \cdot s) \big), \qquad \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( y_i - f_\theta(x_i) \right)^2
   $$

3. **Batch Weighted A\* Search (BWAS):** At inference time, nodes are prioritized by $f(x) = \lambda g(x) + h_\theta(x)$ with $\lambda \in (0, 1]$, popping batches of $B$ nodes simultaneously from the OPEN priority queue to saturate GPU parallel neighbor evaluation.

## Part III: Algebraic Anatomy of Meffert’s Maltese Gear Cube

Meffert’s **Maltese Gear Cube** (conceived by Bram Cohen and designed by Oskar van Deventer) combines planetary gear ratios, a coreless Void-Cube geometry, and a Geared Mixup mechanism.

### 1. Orbit Anatomy ($50$ Movable Components)

1. **8 Hollow Corners (**$C$**):** Act like a $2\times 2\times 2$ Pocket Cube ($G_C \cong S_8 \ltimes \mathbb{Z}_3^7$, or $S_7 \ltimes \mathbb{Z}_3^6$ when normalized).

2. **18 Unified "Ring" Pieces — 12 Edges + 6 Centers (**$R$**):** Because a $90^\circ$ face turn rotates the perpendicular middle-slice ring by $45^\circ$ (half speed), the 12 edge slots and 6 center slots merge into a single **18-slot permutation orbit** ($S_{18}$), where each piece has **4 axial orientations** ($\mathbb{Z}_4$: $0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$).

3. **24 Maltese Cross Gears / Petals (**$P$**):** 4 gears per face ($6 \times 4 = 24$), each with **4 rotational spin states** ($\mathbb{Z}_4$).

### 2. Generator Periods & Why $R' \neq R^3$

Under a single $90^\circ$ clockwise face turn $R \in S_{\text{QTM}}$:

* **Corners (**$C$**):** 4-cycle on the $R$ face (**Order 4**: $R^4\vert{}_C = e$).

* **Ring (**$R$**):** 4-cycle of $R$-face edges plus an **8-cycle** of the $M$-slice ring advancing by $45^\circ$ (**Order 8**: $R^4\vert{}_R \neq e$, $R^8\vert{}_R = e$).

* **Gears (**$P$**):** 4-cycle on the $R$ face plus two parallel 4-cycles on the $M$-slice ring with planetary spin $\Delta \theta \in \mathbb{Z}_4$ (**Order 8/16**).

Consequently, $R^4$ **is NOT the identity**: it leaves corners untouched ($R^4\vert{}_C = e$) while shifting the entire orthogonal $M$-slice ring and its 8 gears by $180^\circ$. Thus $R^{-1} = R^7$ (or $R^{15}$), and $R^4$ **is the fundamental involutive slice generator** in the corner stabilizer subgroup.

### 3. Global Invariants & The 7-Rung Subgroup Ladder

The puzzle group embeds into $G \le (S_8 \ltimes \mathbb{Z}_3^8) \times (S_{18} \ltimes \mathbb{Z}_4^{18}) \times (S_{24} \ltimes \mathbb{Z}_4^{24})$ subject to:

* Corner twist conservation $\sum \omega_C(i) \equiv 0 \pmod 3$.

* **Even Ring Permutation Parity:** Every generator induces a 4-cycle (sign $-1$) and an 8-cycle (sign $-1$) on the 18 ring pieces; since $(-1)(-1) = +1$, $\sigma_R \in A_{18}$.

* Coupled corner-gear-ring spin and quarter-turn edge twist parity.

**Subgroup Factorization Chain (**$G_0 \supset G_1 \supset \dots \supset G_6 = \{e\}$**):**

| Rung | Subgroup Condition | Quotient / Coset Structure | Structural Meaning | 
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | 
| $G_0 \to G_1$ | Solve the $8$ Corners | $G_0 / G_1 \cong S_7 \ltimes \mathbb{Z}_3^6$ ($3,674,160$ states) | Reduces generators to the **Corner-Stabilizer Subgroup** $G_1 = \text{Stab}_G(C)$. | 
| $G_1 \to G_2$ | Solve the $24$ Gears (color + $\mathbb{Z}_4$ spin) | Generated by $R^4$ and corner-preserving conjugates | Places all 4 gears of each color on their home faces with spin $0 \pmod 4$. | 
| $G_2 \to G_3$ | Separate Centers & Edges into home orbits | $\binom{18}{6} = 18,564$ cosets | Returns the $6$ centers to center slots and $12$ edges to edge slots. | 
| $G_3 \to G_4$ | Fix $\mathbb{Z}_2$ Quarter-Turn Edge Parity ("Flush") | $\mathbb{Z}_2^{12}$ coset reduction | Eliminates $90^\circ / 270^\circ$ twisted edges so all ring pieces have twists in $\{0^\circ, 180^\circ\}$. | 
| $G_4 \to G_5$ | Permute the $12$ Edges & $6$ Centers | Subgroup of $(S_{12} \times S_6) \cap A_{18}$ | Places every edge and center into its exact home slot. | 
| $G_5 \to \{e\}$ | Resolve $180^\circ$ Edge Flips | $\mathbb{Z}_2^{11}$ (even-flip subgroup) | Flips pairs of edges $180^\circ$ in place without disturbing gears or centers. | 

### 4. Commutator Cycles & Supporting Macros

1. **Gear 3-Cycle Commutators (**$G_1 \to G_2$**):**

   * Using the 9-move 2-corner swap $A = (R U R' U')(R' F R F')$ and the corner-preserving slice conjugate $X_L = R^4 D R^4 D' R^4 \in G_1$:
     

     $$
     \gamma_1 = A \, X_L \, A^{-1} \, X_L \quad (26 \text{ moves}), \qquad \gamma_2 = A^{-1} \, X_L \, A \, X_L \quad (26 \text{ moves})
     $$

     
     produces a pure 3-cycle of gears ($\text{RF} \to \text{DF} \to \text{DL}$).

   * The 16-move commutator square:
     

     $$
     \gamma_3 = [F R B, R^4]^2 = (F R B \, R^4 \, B' R' F' \, R^4)^2
     $$

     
     cancels order-2 gear swaps and executes a zero-twist 3-cycle of gears.

2. **The 18-Move** $W$ **Macro (**$G_2 \to G_5$**):**
   

   $$
   W = (R^4 U R^4 U')^3
   $$

   * Because $R^4 U R^4 U'$ fixes all corners and induces two disjoint **3-cycles** on the 24 gears, raising it to the **3rd power** annihilates the gear cycles ($W\vert{}_P = e$) while leaving a clean double-transposition $(F \leftrightarrow B)(\text{RF} \leftrightarrow \text{LB})$ and $90^\circ$ twists on the $U$ and $D$ centers.

   * **Center-Edge Orbit Swap:** $F R' \, W \, R F'$.

   * **90° Edge Twist Parity Fix:** $R' U F' \, W \, F U' R$.

3. **The 42-Move Pure Edge Flip (**$W^2$ **Conjugate):**

   * Squaring $W$ ($W^2 = (R^4 U R^4 U')^6$, 36 moves) cancels the $(F \leftrightarrow B)$ and $(\text{RF} \leftrightarrow \text{LB})$ swaps and isolates pure $180^\circ$ flips. Conjugating by $g = U' R F'$ places the $\text{DL}$ edge into the active flip slot while hiding other flips on mono-colored centers:
     

     $$
     \text{Flip}_{\text{DL}} = (U' R F') \, (R^4 U R^4 U')^6 \, (F R' U) \quad (42 \text{ moves})
     $$

## Part IV: Algebraic Hostility & Hierarchical Coset Value Iteration

### 1. What Does "Algebraically Hostile" Mean?

A puzzle is **algebraically hostile** when its group $G$ and generating set $S$ resist quotient projection and local commutator construction across four dimensions:

1. **Multi-Orbit Planetary Locking (Zero Clean Quotients):** Every primitive generator $s \in S$ scrambles all orbits simultaneously at incommensurate periods ($4, 8, 16$).

2. **Orbit Conflation:** Centers and edges merge into $S_{18}$, destroying the fixed spatial reference frame.

3. **Destructive Cycle-Cancellation Bottlenecks:** Short words induce overlapping cycles of lengths $2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8$ across gears and ring pieces; raising words to powers to cancel gear cycles often kills the desired ring action.

4. **Extreme Subgroup & Metric Distortion:** The embedding $\text{Stab}_G(C, P) \hookrightarrow G$ has massive distortion—states differing by a single edge flip (Hamming distance $2$) sit at word distance $d_S(x, e) = 42$ in $\text{Cay}(G, S)$, creating severe Lipschitz discontinuities ("value cliffs") for a monolithic neural network.

### 2. Rung-by-Rung Coset Value Iteration & Intermediate Data Generation

To eliminate value cliffs and the $\sim 100$-move mixing horizon, we train an independent value network $V_{\theta_i}: G_i / G_{i+1} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ on each **Schreier coset graph** $\Gamma_i = \text{Sch}(G_i, G_{i+1}, S_i)$:

#### Canonical Coset Projection ($\pi_i$)

For a state $x \in G_i$, the projection $\pi_i(x)$ clamps upstream solved orbits ($< i$) to identity, extracts the active orbit of Rung $i$, and **masks out (zeros) all downstream unsolved orbits (**$> i$**)**:
*Because the right action of* $S_i \subset G_i$ *on cosets* $G_{i+1} \backslash G_i$ *is well-defined (*$\pi_i(x) = \pi_i(y) \implies \pi_i(x \cdot m) = \pi_i(y \cdot m)$*), reverse random walks for Rung* $i$ *can start directly from the global identity* $e$*!*

#### Two Methods for Intermediate Rung Data Generation ($G_i \to G_{i+1}$)

1. **Method A: Subgroup-Closed Macro-Walks (**$S_i \subset G_i$**):**

   * Construct a symmetrized macro set $S_i$ by conjugating base stabilizers (e.g., $R^4, \gamma_1, \gamma_2, \gamma_3$ for Rung 1, or $W$ conjugates for Rung 2) by all 24 spatial cube rotations $\rho \in \mathcal{R} \cong S_4$.

   * Start at $x_0 = e$, sample walk depth $K \sim \text{Uniform}(1, K_{\max}^{(i)})$, and step via vectorized permutation lookups $x_t = x_{t-1} \circ \text{perm}(m_t)$ for $m_t \in S_i$.

   * Train $V_{\theta_i}$ via length-weighted Bellman backups:
     

     $$
     y_i(\pi_i(x_t)) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \pi_i(x_t) = \pi_i(e) \quad (x_t \in G_{i+1}) \\ \min_{m \in S_i} \Big( \Vert{}m\Vert{}_{S_0} + V_{\theta_i^-}\big(\pi_i(x_t \cdot m)\big) \Big) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
     $$

2. **Method B: Two-Stage Stratified Reverse Sampling (Primitive Excursion Walks):**

   * To allow a network to use primitive moves $S_0$ that temporarily break and re-solve earlier rungs without suffering distribution shift:

     * **Stage 1:** Walk $M$ steps from $e$ using macros $m \in S_i$ to land on a random manifold state $z_0 \in G_i$.

     * **Stage 2:** Take a short $k$-step primitive excursion walk ($k \le 10$) in $S_0$ from $z_0$ to populate the tubular neighborhood around $G_i$ inside $\text{Cay}(G_0, S_0)$.

## Part V: Automated Discovery of Subgroup Ladders for Unknown Puzzles

Given an unknown puzzle specified only as raw generator permutations $S_0 = \{s_1, \dots, s_k\}$ on $N$ points, an automated solver discovers and orders the rungs $G_0 \supset G_1 \supset \dots \supset G_m = \{e\}$ in two phases:

### Phase 1: Discovering Candidate Rung Projections

1. **Transitive Orbit Decomposition:** Connected components of $\{1, \dots, N\}$ under $S_0$ partition the puzzle into disjoint physical orbits $\Omega = \Omega_1 \sqcup \dots \sqcup \Omega_r$ (e.g., Corners, Gears, Ring). Each kernel $\text{Stab}_G(\Omega_j)$ is a major rung candidate.

2. **Imprimitive Block Systems (Atkinson’s Algorithm):** Within each orbit $\Omega_j$, running Atkinson's algorithm in $O(\vert{}\Omega_j\vert{}^2)$ time extracts minimal and maximal block systems $\mathcal{B}$:

   * Grouping orientation stickers on the same piece separates **Permutation** from **Orientation**.

   * Subgroup blocks inside cyclic orientation groups (e.g., $2\mathbb{Z}_4 \subset \mathbb{Z}_4$) automatically discover intermediate parity rungs (such as flush $\{0^\circ, 180^\circ\}$ vs. twisted $\{90^\circ, 270^\circ\}$ edges).

   * Generator fixed-point sets identify setwise stabilizer sub-orbits (separating the 6 center slots from the 12 edge slots in $\Omega_R$).

### Phase 2: Quantitative Rules for Ordering the Rungs

1. **The Period-Exponent Hierarchy (Free Stabilizers):**
   Compute the generator period $p_j(s) = \text{ord}(\phi_j(s))$ on each orbit. **Always order orbits in ascending order of their generator periods:**
   

   $$
   p(\Omega_{\text{Rung } 0}) < p(\Omega_{\text{Rung } 1}) \le p(\Omega_{\text{Rung } 2})
   $$

   
   Because $p_{\text{Corners}}(R) = 4$ divides $p_{\text{Ring}}(R) = 8$, solving Corners first gives $R^4 \in \text{Stab}_G(\Omega_C)$ for free. Solving the Ring first would require $R^8$, which simultaneously kills the Corners and yields zero free generators.

2. **Commutator Support Sparsity & Kernel Hit Rate:**
   Run a bounded BFS/random sieve in $S_i$ and check how quickly small powers $w^p$ or commutators $[w_1, w_2]$ fall into $\ker(\phi_j)$ while acting non-trivially on remaining orbits (e.g., $(R^4 U R^4 U')^3$ kills gear 3-cycles in 18 moves while transforming the ring, dictating $\text{Gears} \to \text{Ring}$).

3. **Normal Subgroup Structure Within Wreath Products:**
   In a wreath product $S_n \ltimes \mathbb{Z}_d^n$, the pure orientation base group $N = \{e\} \ltimes \mathbb{Z}_2^{n-1}$ is an **abelian normal subgroup**. Saving pure in-place $180^\circ$ flips for the final rung ensures that conjugating a single flip macro $g M_{\text{flip}} g^{-1}$ by any setup word $g$ stays inside $N$ without disturbing piece positions.

4. **Greedy Rung Efficiency Objective:**
   At step $i$, filter candidate projections $\pi \in \Pi$ with tractable index $[G_i : \text{Stab}_{G_i}(\pi)] \in [2, B_{\max}]$, harvest stabilizing macros $S_{\text{next}}(\pi)$ via Schreier coset collisions ($w_1 w_2^{-1} \in G_{i+1}$) and cycle-order sieving, and maximize:
   

   $$
   \text{Score}(\pi) = \frac{\log_2 [G_i : \text{Stab}_{G_i}(\pi)]}{\frac{1}{\vert{}S_{\text{next}}(\pi)\vert{}} \sum_{m \in S_{\text{next}}(\pi)} \Vert{}m\Vert{}_{S_0}}
   $$

   
   This objective maximizes the bits of puzzle entropy resolved per rung while minimizing the primitive move length of the macro-generators passed down to the next rung.
